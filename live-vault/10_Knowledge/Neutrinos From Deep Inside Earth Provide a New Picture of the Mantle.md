overview:
A global network of neutrino detectors is creating a new view of radioactive elements that power Earth’s tectonic heat engine. The JUNO experiment in China is expected to report its first geoneutrino detections in 2026. Neutrinos, which rarely interact with matter, offer insights into Earth’s interior, particularly the mantle and core, by detecting radioactive decay signatures from uranium, thorium, and potassium. These findings help refine geological models and understand Earth’s thermal evolution.

details:
- **JUNO Experiment**: Located in Guangzhou, China, JUNO is designed to detect geoneutrinos, which are emitted during radioactive decay in Earth’s crust and mantle. The experiment is expected to provide new data on the distribution of heat sources within the planet.
- **SNO+ Detector**: A neutrino experiment at Snolab in Canada, SNO+ uses a 780-ton liquid scintillator to detect geoneutrinos. The detector is shielded from cosmic radiation to enhance its sensitivity to rare interactions.
- **KamLAND Collaboration**: This Japanese experiment, based on the Kamioka Liquid-scintillator Antineutrino Detector, has been instrumental in measuring geoneutrinos from radioactive decay. Its data has improved estimates of Earth’s internal heat sources, showing that radioactive decay contributes about half of Earth’s heat.
- **Borexino Experiment**: This experiment has gathered data on geoneutrinos, offering constraints on geological models of the mantle. It has also helped identify the role of potassium in Earth’s thermal history.
- **Geo-neutrino Detection**: Most previous measurements have relied on inverse beta decay, which is insensitive to potassium. New methods using neutrino-electron elastic scattering and direction-sensitive detectors aim to measure potassium contributions, which could provide insights into Earth’s early thermal evolution.

unresolved:
- **Potassium Contribution**: The geo-neutrino flux from potassium decay has not been measured due to its energy being below the detection threshold of inverse beta decay. Measuring this flux could reveal more about Earth’s internal composition and thermal history.
- **Magma Systems**: A large magma reservoir was discovered beneath Tuscany using seismic imaging, highlighting the potential for geophysical methods to uncover hidden geological structures. This discovery could also aid in locating geothermal and mineral resources.
